What Is the Next Scaling After Model Scaling?

For daily users, the knowledge layer of top-tier chatbots like ChatGPT and Claude is already good enough for many jobs. The next product frontier is cheaper, faster, more agentic automation — and that means context scaling and agent scaling. Both demand significantly more memory bandwidth, which means the HBM shortage is far from over.

Gordon Guocheng Qian
June 22, 2026 · 9 min read

For most daily users, the baseline knowledge and reasoning in products like ChatGPT and Claude already feels good enough for many everyday jobs: answering questions, writing code, summarizing documents, planning trips, drafting emails, and explaining concepts. Once that capability becomes normal, the user’s unmet need shifts from “know more” to “do more for me.” Haters will say we just need more parameters. XD

Context scaling

Today’s tier-0 models already ship with context windows around 1M tokens. That is enough to upload an entire book, ask questions about it, run a web search, and aggregate everything before generating a thorough response.

But if you keep uploading books, keep attaching images, keep searching the internet, keep talking with the chatbot across a long session — sooner or later you hit the limit. The context fills up, earlier information gets evicted or compressed, and the model’s quality degrades. This is the most obvious reason to scale context further.

But there is a deeper reason, and it is more important.

Context is an alternative to gradient descent.

This line comes from Andrej Karpathy’s conversation with Dwarkesh Patel. The deeper point is not that the context window is merely a long-term memory buffer, but that it gives the model another way to adapt at inference time.

The precise version is not that the model’s weights change during inference. They do not. The point is that the context can behave like a temporary learning substrate: examples, retrieved documents, notes, tests, plans, tool outputs, and failure traces all become state that changes the model’s behavior during the forward pass.

This connects to Jiayi Weng’s Learning Beyond Gradients: a coding agent can keep reading failures, editing code, adding tests, watching replays, and improving a software system without training a new neural network. The object being updated is explicit and maintainable — rules, tests, logs, memory, version diffs, and feedback channels — rather than only hidden weights.

While in-context learning is primarily advanced pattern completion, it may act mechanically like a small, internal gradient-descent loop running during the network’s forward pass.
— Andrej Karpathy

Recursive self-improvement might require no training — only In-Context Manipulation. An agent that can write to its own context — notes, plans, past mistakes, discovered heuristics, sub-goals, intermediate reasoning — can effectively “fine-tune” itself at inference time without touching a single weight. The larger and more structured the context, the more powerful this self-improvement loop becomes.

Customization requires no training — only larger context.

This is also why larger context windows matter for enterprise and personalized LLMs. Instead of fine-tuning a separate model for every customer, you can condition a single strong model on a rich, persistent context: user preferences, company knowledge base, interaction history, domain-specific instructions. Personalization becomes a context-engineering problem rather than a training problem.

Agent scaling

The second scaling dimension is agent count.

This is already the direction of the market. Top-tier chatbot companies are not only launching larger flagship models; they are also shipping cheaper, faster models and wrapping them in more agentic products: coding agents, deep research, computer-use agents, workflow automation, and task execution. The trend is clear: most users are increasingly satisfied with raw model capability, but they want more automation.

The token evidence points in the same direction. As models become cheaper and more useful, the market does not merely consume a little more inference. It consumes orders of magnitude more tokens — first through chat and API usage, then through agents that read, search, plan, call tools, verify outputs, and retry in loops.

Current agent systems are still early: one model, one context, one stream of actions.

Cathie Wood and ARK Invest make a similar point in their Big Ideas 2026 report. They describe foundation models as a new layer of the internet stack: users will interact less by clicking through apps and more by delegating goals to AI agents that search, compare, purchase, and transact on their behalf. In their framing, agents become a kind of consumer operating system.

Now imagine a future release of ChatGPT or Claude Code where you can spin up 100, or 1,000 agents simultaneously — each one with superhuman-level intelligence in its domain, each one with its own context, running in parallel, coordinating through shared memory or message passing.

  • One agent reads the codebase; another writes tests; another reviews the diff; another deploys; another monitors logs.
  • One agent searches the literature; another runs experiments; another writes the paper; another manages citations.
  • One agent monitors your portfolio; another scans filings; another executes trades; another handles risk.

The compute demand does not scale linearly with agent count — it scales with agent count × context length × coordination overhead. A 100-agent system where each agent maintains a 1M-token context is a fundamentally different compute profile from a single chatbot.

Both require significantly more HBM — and more AI infrastructure

Here is the hardware reality:

  • Context scaling means the KV cache grows linearly with context length. A 1M-token context on a 10T-parameter model requires enormous memory just to store activations. Going to 10M or 100M tokens does not just need bigger context — it needs bigger memory per GPU, and more of it.
  • Agent scaling means running many independent model instances in parallel, each with its own KV cache. 1,000 agents × 1M tokens each is 1 billion tokens of live context that must reside in fast memory.

Both of these are memory-bandwidth-bound and memory-capacity-bound problems first. But once context and agents scale, the demand does not stop at HBM. It spills into custom inference silicon, scale-out networking, scale-up interconnect, optical DSPs, and full-stack GPU systems.

So the investment thesis is simple:

Model scaling drove the last wave of GPU demand. Context scaling and agent scaling will drive the next wave of memory, networking, custom ASIC, optical interconnect, and full-stack AI infrastructure demand.

Summary

Model scaling still matters. But for daily users, the practical bottleneck is shifting: the chatbot already knows enough for many tasks, while the product still does too little on the user’s behalf.

Context scaling is the next scaling law because context can act as an inference-time learning substrate — not by updating weights, but by carrying examples, memory, tests, and feedback that manipulate behavior during inference.

Agent scaling is the obvious next step: from one chatbot response to hundreds or thousands of automated actions, each handled by cheaper, faster, specialized agents running in parallel.

Both demand dramatically more memory and much more infrastructure around the model. The bottleneck shifts from pure FLOPS to HBM bandwidth and capacity, inference ASICs, networking, optical interconnect, and scale-up systems. So the conclusion of this blog is simple:

Buy more SK Hynix & Micron. 😂

This is not really a joke. In the context-scaling + agent-scaling thesis, memory / HBM is the most upstream constraint, and the leaders are clear: SK Hynix is the HBM leader with roughly 50–60% share; Micron is the cleanest U.S.-listed HBM / DRAM vehicle, competing for the second or third position in HBM and recently overtaking Samsung in DRAM; Samsung is the third major HBM player.

But the beneficiary map is broader than HBM. By category, the leaders look like this:

  • Memory / HBM: SK Hynix is the upstream leader for the context + agent scaling stack, with roughly 50–60% HBM share. Micron is the cleanest U.S.-listed way to express the HBM / DRAM thesis: HBM second or third, and DRAM recently ahead of Samsung. Samsung is the third HBM player.
  • Custom ASIC / XPU: this is the second wave of inference and agent compute. Broadcom is the leader, with roughly 70% share of custom AI accelerator silicon; Marvell is the second major player at roughly 25%. Broadcom’s AI business is scaling extremely fast: Q1 FY2026 AI revenue was around $8.4B, up 106% year over year; AI chip gross margin is roughly 65%; FY2026 AI revenue target is around $56B, with backlog around $73B and a 2027 ambition near $100B. Its XPU customer map reportedly includes Google, Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic, Apple, and possibly ByteDance.
  • AI networking switch silicon: Broadcom is also the leader here. Tomahawk 6 is the industry’s first 102.4 Tbps Ethernet switch chip, and Jericho 4 extends the fabric layer for massive AI clusters.
  • Optical interconnect / optical DSP: Marvell leads the optical DSP layer. Its PAM4 DSPs are the industry standard behind 800G and 1.6T optical modules, which become more important as scale-out AI clusters keep expanding.
  • Merchant Ethernet switch systems: Arista is the system-level leader. Underneath, many of those switches still rely on Broadcom silicon, while NVIDIA’s Spectrum-X is aggressively chasing the AI Ethernet opportunity.
  • GPU / full-stack AI compute: NVIDIA remains the full-stack leader with roughly 70% AI chip share. With Vera + NVLink, it is not only the GPU leader; it is also leading the agent-CPU and scale-up interconnect layer.
